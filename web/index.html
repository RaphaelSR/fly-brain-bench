What loads

Every neuron, in its real place

A fly brain was sliced into 7,000 layers, photographed under an electron microscope, and traced by machine and by hand. The result is 138,639 neurons and 2,700,513 connections between them — measured, not invented.

What a neuron does

It is a bucket with a hole in it

Signals arriving fill the bucket. It leaks back down on its own. If the level crosses a line, the neuron fires — it empties, and squirts into every bucket it is wired to. That is the whole rule. Nothing here is trained, and nothing learns.

Pick a sense on the left — sugar on the mouthparts, the smell of rotting fruit, a shadow growing overhead. Those neurons start firing at 150 Hz, and the activity spreads outward along the real wiring. Nobody tells it where to go.

What is actually happening here

1 The map is real, and it was photographed

A female fruit fly's brain was cut into about 7,000 slices and imaged with an electron microscope. Machine learning traced every neuron through the stack, then people checked and corrected it — millions of edits. What came out is not a model of a brain. It is a map of one particular brain: which of the 138,639 neurons touches which, and how many times.

Published by the FlyWire consortium, release 783. Free to reuse under CC-BY.

2 Each neuron is a leaky bucket

Every neuron here holds a voltage. Left alone it drifts back to its resting level of −52 mV, like water draining out. Incoming signals push it up — or down, if the sender is an inhibitory neuron. Cross −45 mV and it fires: it dumps its charge into everything downstream, resets to the bottom, and goes deaf for 2.2 ms before it can fire again.

Five constants, taken from published fly electrophysiology. No weights were fitted, and there is no training step anywhere in this page.

3 Colour is the chemical, not the mood

Each neuron releases one transmitter, and that decides whether it pushes its targets up or pulls them down. Acetylcholine excites; GABA and glutamate inhibit. In this fly, 62% of neurons are excitatory. The colours in the viewport are those chemicals — so a wash of amber spreading outward is excitation, and the blue and violet cells are the brakes.

4 Stimulating is the only input

When you choose a sense, the neurons that carry it are driven with random pulses at 150 Hz — the electrical equivalent of holding sugar against the mouthparts. Everything after that is the wiring doing its own thing. Activity is not routed, scored, or steered. It spreads or it dies out, depending on what those neurons happen to be connected to.

The sugar preset reproduces the benchmark experiment from the paper this model comes from: drive the taste neurons, and the motor neurons that extend the proboscis light up a few milliseconds later.

5 What this is not

  • The map gives wiring, not physiology. Real synapse strengths, neuromodulation and plasticity were never measured — here, strength is just the number of contacts.
  • No learning, no memory of past runs, no behaviour. It reacts and then settles.
  • Connections of fewer than 5 contacts are dropped to keep the download to 8 MB. Against the full model on the sugar benchmark this keeps 92.5% of the responding neurons and a rate correlation of 0.988.

· Why a fly, and why it fits on a laptop

A human brain has roughly 86 billion neurons. This one has 139 thousand — small enough to map completely, and small enough that the entire wiring diagram compresses to less than a photograph. It is currently the largest brain of any animal that has been mapped connection by connection.
